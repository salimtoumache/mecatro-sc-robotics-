A robot is a system

A real robot is never only kinematics. It is a system where mechanics, electronics, software, safety and the application must work together. Most failures of robot projects do not come from a wrong inverse kinematics, but from a gripper that does not grip, a cable that breaks, a missing safety study or a badly chosen robot.

This last course follows the life of a robot project: choose the family (1), design the body (2, 3), power and wire it (4), program it (5), make it safe (6), calibrate and test it (7), deploy it for an application (8) and grow your skills (9).

1.1 Two ideas that explain most families

Fixed or mobile base. A fixed-base arm knows exactly where its base is: kinematics gives the tool pose directly. A mobile robot must also find where its base is (localisation, see the “Perception” course).

Actuated or underactuated. A robot is underactuated when it has fewer actuators than degrees of freedom. A quadrotor has 6 DOF in space but only 4 motors: it cannot move sideways without first tilting. A walking robot is underactuated at the foot that touches the ground. It must be stabilised dynamically, which is why balance control is a whole field.

1.2 Flying: the quadrotor

To hover, the sum of the four thrusts equals the weight: T = m g / 4 per rotor. To tilt forward, speed up the rear rotors; to turn (yaw), speed up the two rotors that spin in one direction and slow the other two (the reaction torques no longer cancel).

Worked example

A 1.2 kg drone: weight 1.2 × 9.81 = 11.8 N, so 2.9 N per rotor to hover. To climb at 2 m/s² (acceleration of 0.2 g) the total thrust must be 1.2 × (9.81 + 2) = 14.2 N: 20 % more. Motors are therefore chosen to give at least twice the hover thrust (thrust-to-weight ratio of 2) to keep control margin in wind.

1.3 Walking: the support polygon

A legged robot is statically stable if the vertical projection of its centre of mass lies inside the support polygon (the area between its feet on the ground). A quadruped that always keeps three feet down moves with a very safe “static walk”; a trot or a run lifts feet, the polygon becomes a line or disappears, and the robot is only dynamically stable, like a human running. Walking controllers use simple models: the inverted pendulum (the body falls forward and the foot is placed to catch it) and the zero moment point (ZMP), then whole-body control or learned policies on top.

2.2 First sizing: the torque at the base of the arm

The shoulder torque is a weight times its distance. For a quick estimate, take the payload at full reach plus half the weight of the arm (its centre of mass is about halfway):

τ ≈ ( mpayload + ½ marm ) g L

Worked example

A 5 kg payload at L = 0.8 m, with an arm of 8 kg: τ ≈ (5 + 4) × 9.81 × 0.8 ≈ 71 N·m at the shoulder, before any acceleration. With an acceleration of 3 rad/s² and a total inertia of about 5 kg·m², add 15 N·m. With a 100:1 gearbox of efficiency 0.8, the motor must give 86 / (100 × 0.8) ≈ 1.1 N·m at peak: a larger motor than the wrist ones. This shows why shoulders are heavy and why big robots have counterbalance springs.

2.3 Stiffness: why accuracy is not repeatability

Links bend. For a beam fixed at one end, the deflection under a force F at the free end is:

δ = F L³ / (3 E I)

with E the Young modulus of the material (aluminium 70 GPa, steel 200 GPa, carbon fibre 100 to 200 GPa) and I the second moment of area (for a tube I = π (D⁴ − d⁴) / 64). Note the cube: doubling the length multiplies the deflection by 8.

Worked example

An aluminium tube, outer diameter 40 mm, inner 36 mm, length 0.5 m, with a load of 20 N at its end: I = π (0.04⁴ − 0.036⁴) / 64 = 4.3·10⁻⁸ m⁴ and δ = 20 × 0.5³ / (3 × 70·10⁹ × 4.3·10⁻⁸) ≈ 0.28 mm. The robot is repeatable (it bends the same way every time) but the tool is 0.28 mm away from where the encoders say: that is a typical origin of the difference between repeatability and accuracy.

    Multiplied by 2³ = 8.

  2. Why is a robot repeatable to 0.02 mm sometimes 0.3 mm inaccurate?

    Because of deflection, geometric errors and backlash, which are the same each time (repeatable) but not in the model.

3.1 How much force to hold an object?

Friction grip. Two fingers press with a normal force N each; the friction coefficient is μ. To lift a mass m with a safety factor SF that covers acceleration and uncertainty:

2 μ N ≥ SF · m g so N ≥ SF · m g / (2 μ)

Worked example

A 1 kg part, μ = 0.3 (steel on a dry finger pad), SF = 2: N ≥ 2 × 1 × 9.81 / (2 × 0.3) ≈ 33 N per finger. With a rubber pad (μ ≈ 1) only 10 N are needed: a good pad saves a gripper size. A form-closure grip (fingers shaped to the part, or a part held by a groove) needs almost no friction.

Vacuum. The holding force is the pressure difference times the cup area: F = ΔP × A. With a vacuum of 60 kPa and a cup of 30 mm diameter (A = 7.07·10⁻⁴ m²): F = 60 000 × 7.07·10⁻⁴ ≈ 42 N. With a safety factor of 4 for a horizontal suction (the load slides) or fast moves, one cup carries about 42 / (4 × 9.81) ≈ 1.1 kg. For a 10 kg box, use several cups, or a larger area.

3.2 The tool frame and TCP

The robot knows the pose of its flange (the mounting face). The point that works (the tip of a torch, the middle of the gripper fingers) is the tool centre point (TCP), described by a fixed transform flange → TCP (a frame, as in the modelling course). Programs are written in terms of the TCP, so it must be calibrated: the usual 4-point method touches the same fixed tip from four different orientations and the controller solves for the offset. A wrong TCP is invisible in joint moves but makes the tool “swing around” the wrong point when you reorient it. The payload data (mass, centre of mass, inertia) of the tool must also be entered: it feeds the dynamic model (control course).

Remember
  • The gripper is often the real limit of a robot project: choose it first, with the part, not last.
  • Test grasps with the real parts, at the real speed and acceleration.
  • Release is as important as grasp: pushing air to release a part, or a spring to open on power loss, avoids dropping or sticking.

Electronics and embedded systems: power, drivers, controllers, buses

13 min
You will learn

what electronics a robot contains, how to size the power supply, and which controller and bus to choose.

4.1 Power: batteries and supplies

  • Battery energy = voltage × capacity: a 24 V, 10 Ah pack stores 240 Wh. The runtime is the energy divided by the average power: 240 Wh / 60 W = 4 h. Keep 20 % in reserve.
  • Chemistries: Li-ion / LiPo (high energy density, needs a battery management system, BMS), LiFePO₄ (safer, longer life), lead-acid (cheap, heavy), NiMH.
  • Current: I = P / V. A 100 W motor at 24 V draws 4.2 A; at 12 V it draws 8.3 A, so thicker wires and bigger connectors. Higher voltage means less current and less loss (Ploss = R I²).
  • Peaks: motors draw several times their average current when accelerating; the supply and the wires must tolerate it, and braking can push energy back (use a brake resistor or a regenerative supply).

4.2 Drivers, controllers and buses

LayerOptionsChoose when
Motor driverH-bridge (brushed), FOC driver (brushless, with current sensing and encoder input), stepper driver (microstepping), industrial servo drive.Match the motor type, voltage and current; add protections.
Microcontroller (MCU)Arduino, STM32, ESP32, Teensy.Hard real time, low cost and power: fast loops, sensors, motor control.
Single-board computerRaspberry Pi, Jetson (GPU), industrial PC.Vision, planning, ROS 2, AI: high layers, with Linux.

Bus / networkI²C, SPI, UART (on a board); CAN / CAN-FD (robust, shared cable, 1 Mbit/s); EtherCAT (fast, synchronous, industrial); USB, Ethernet, Wi-Fi.CAN for a few joints on a small robot; EtherCAT for many axes with tight timing.

4.3 Signals and noise

  • ADC resolution: a 12-bit converter with a 3.3 V range has a step of 3.3 / 4096 ≈ 0.8 mV. A sensor of 10 mV per unit then has a resolution of 0.08 unit.
  • PWM: a motor is driven by switching the voltage at 10 to 20 kHz (above hearing); the duty cycle sets the average voltage.
  • EMC: motor drivers create electrical noise that corrupts encoder and sensor signals. Use shielded twisted-pair cables, a single ground point, separate power and signal cables, filters, and differential signalling (RS-422, CAN).
  • Protections: fuses or breakers, reverse-polarity and over-current protection, limit switches, temperature sensors on the motors and drivers.
Never forget the safety circuit

An emergency stop must not depend on the software. It cuts the power to the drives through certified relays or safe-torque-off inputs (see lesson 6). Test it every time you modify the robot.

  1. A 12 V, 5 Ah battery powers a robot that draws 30 W on average. How long does it run, keeping 20 % in reserve?

    Energy 60 Wh × 0.8 = 48 Wh; 48 / 30 = 1.6 h.

  2. Why is a higher supply voltage often chosen for powerful motors?

    For the same power the current is lower, so thinner wires and less loss (R I²).

  3. Which bus would you pick to link 12 synchronised servo drives?

Remember the reality gap: a simulator always lacks some friction, flexibility or sensor noise; test on the real robot early.

5.4 Good engineering habits

  • Version control (git) for code, parameters and robot programs; backups of the controller.
  • Test in layers: unit tests, simulation, hardware in the loop, then the real robot at low speed.
  • Logs and monitoring: record everything; failures are rare and hard to reproduce.
  • Error handling: every action can fail; define what the robot does then (retry, stop safely, call for help).
  • Security: a networked robot is a computer: passwords, updates, closed ports (IEC 62443).

6

Safety and standards: working next to robots

13 min
You will learn

the hazards of robots, the main standards, and the four ways of letting people and robots share a space.

Important

This lesson explains the ideas. A real installation needs a risk assessment by a competent person and compliance with the standards and laws of the country. Never rely on a course alone for safety decisions.

6.1 Where the danger comes from

  • Impact: a 100 kg arm moving at 1.5 m/s has a kinetic energy ½ m v² of 112 J, enough to injure seriously.
  • Crushing and trapping between the robot and a fixed object (quasi-static contact): the force does not stop at the first touch.
  • Tool hazards: sharp parts, hot welding torches, lasers, a gripper that releases its load, ejected parts.
  • Unexpected motion: a software error, a restart, a singularity (!), a person entering during a maintenance step.
  • Other: electrical, pneumatic and hydraulic energy, noise, cybersecurity.

Worked example: the separation distance (mode 3)

The robot must stop before a person reaches it. The minimum protective distance is the sum of: the distance a person walks while the system detects and stops (Sh = Kh · (Tr + Ts), with the human speed Kh = 1.6 m/s in ISO/TS 15066), the distance the robot travels while stopping, the detection uncertainty and a margin. With a total reaction and stopping time of 0.5 s, the person covers 1.6 × 0.5 = 0.8 m before the robot stops: the scanner must trigger more than 0.8 m plus the robot stopping distance and margins away. A slower or faster-stopping robot allows a smaller separation.

6.4 The protective devices

  • Fences and interlocked doors, light curtains, laser scanners, pressure mats, safety cameras.
  • Emergency stop (red mushroom button, yellow background): stop categories 0 (immediate power removal), 1 (controlled stop then power removal), 2 (controlled stop, power kept). Enabling switch (three-position) on the pendant.
  • Safe functions in the drives: safe torque off (STO), safely limited speed (SLS), safe limited position, safe brake control. They are certified and independent of the standard software.
  • Operating modes: automatic, and manual reduced speed for teaching (limited to 250 mm/s at the tool in the “T1” mode).

Joint errors reach the tool through the Jacobian (dx = J dq, singularities course): the longer the arm, the larger the effect. An error of 0.01° on the shoulder joint is 0.01 × π/180 = 1.75·10⁻⁴ rad; at 1 m this is 0.17 mm at the tool. A well-conditioned pose keeps this amplification small.

Accuracy versus repeatability, again

Calibration improves accuracy, not repeatability. A robot that is repeatable to ±0.03 mm but 1 mm inaccurate can be taught points by hand and will return to them precisely (teach and repeat). It needs calibration when points come from a CAD model, a vision system, or when a robot is replaced: the program is then sent to a robot with different errors.

7.3 Testing: ISO 9283

The standard ISO 9283 defines how to measure a robot so that data sheets can be compared: the robot goes to the same pose many times (typically 30 cycles) from different directions, and a measuring instrument records where the tool really goes.

MeasureWhat it says
Pose accuracy (AP)Distance between the commanded pose and the mean of the reached poses.
Pose repeatability (RP)Spread of the reached poses around their mean (a sphere that contains them).
Path accuracy and repeatability (AT, RT)The same along a programmed line or circle, at a given speed.
Settling time, overshootHow long the tool takes to stabilise at the target.
Commissioning checklist
  1. Mount the robot rigidly; check the levelling and the fixings.
  2. Check the mastering, the payload and the tool frame (TCP).
  3. Warm the robot up (one hour of motion) before accuracy-critical work.
  4. Run at low speed first, then increase; watch the motor currents.
  5. Test the safety functions (emergency stop, doors, scanners) and record the results.
  6. Back up the program and the parameters.

Check yourself

  1. The shoulder encoder is wrong by 0.02°. The tool is 1.2 m away. What is the error at the tool?

    0.02° = 3.5·10⁻⁴ rad; × 1.2 m = 0.42 mm.

  2. A robot has been taught its points by hand. Does it need kinematic calibration?

    Not necessarily: repeatability is enough to return to taught points. Calibration matters when points come from CAD or vision, or the robot is replaced.

  3. Why warm a robot up before a precise task?

    Thermal expansion of links and lubricant changes the geometry in the first hour; a warm robot is more stable.

8.1 A method to choose

  1. Define the task in numbers: what is handled (mass, size, material, variety), how many per hour, what precision, what environment (dust, water, temperature, cleanroom, explosive atmosphere).
  2. Choose the kinematics: 4 DOF pick and place on a plane → SCARA or delta; a box-shaped workspace → Cartesian gantry; any pose in space → 6-axis arm; moving across a site → mobile robot or mobile manipulator.
  3. Check the numbers: payload (with gripper and a 30 % margin), reach (with the whole workspace inside it and away from singularities), repeatability (about one third of the tolerance), cycle time (estimate it; see below).
  4. Decide the safety concept: fenced, light curtain, scanner, or collaborative (lesson 6). A cobot is slower: check that the cycle time is still enough.
  5. Plan the whole cell: gripper, feeding of parts, fixtures, vision, PLC and software, safety, guarding, maintenance access.
  6. Simulate the cell (reachability, collisions, cycle time) and test the risky step (the grasp, the vision) on a prototype before ordering.

8.2 Estimating the cycle time

Add the moves, the gripper actions and the waiting: a pick and place cycle is roughly 2 × (approach + grasp or release + retract) + transfer. A fast pick and place of 0.5 m with accelerations of 10 m/s²: time for a move with a triangle profile = 2 √(d/a) = 2 √(0.5/10) ≈ 0.45 s.

Worked example: payback of a robot cell
  • A palletising cell must handle 600 boxes per hour: a cycle of 3600 / 600 = 6 s per box (2 boxes at a time would give 12 s per gripper cycle).
  • Cost: robot 40 000 €, gripper, safety, conveyors and integration 80 000 €, total 120 000 €. Rule of thumb: the integration costs 1.5 to 3 times the robot.
  • Savings: two operators on two shifts replaced, 60 000 € per year, minus 6 000 € of maintenance: 54 000 € per year.
  • Payback = 120 000 / 54 000 ≈ 2.2 years. Include the non-financial gains: fewer injuries, constant quality, availability.

8.3 Why robot projects fail

  • The gripper or the feeding of parts was not tested with real, variable parts.
  • The variety was underestimated: the robot is good at the same task a million times, bad at everything that changes.
  • Integration and software cost more than the robot, and nobody planned the maintenance or the training.
  • The process was not ready for automation: a robot automates a good process, it does not repair a bad one.
  • The safety study was done too late, forcing a redesign.

9.2 A ladder of projects

  1. Servo arm (Arduino + 3 hobby servos): write the forward and inverse kinematics of a 2-3 axis arm and make it draw.
  2. Line follower or balancing robot: your first PID, sensors and motor drivers.
  3. Differential-drive rover with encoders and an IMU: odometry, then a Kalman filter.
  4. ROS 2 in simulation: a TurtleBot in Gazebo with SLAM Toolbox and Nav2; then the same on a real robot with a cheap lidar.
  5. Pick and place: a 5-6 axis arm (or a simulated UR5) with MoveIt, a camera and ArUco markers, hand-eye calibration included.
  6. Learning: train a policy in simulation (MuJoCo / Isaac Lab) and test it on a small real robot; or learn from demonstrations.
  7. A complete system: a mobile manipulator or a dual-arm cell with a safety concept, a risk assessment and documentation. This is the “hero” project.

9.3 Competitions and communities

  • Student and youth: FIRST Robotics, RoboCup and RoboCup Junior, World Robot Olympiad, Eurobot, VEX.
  • Research challenges: DARPA challenges, ICRA / IROS competitions, Amazon picking, RoboMaster, autonomous racing.
  • Communities: ROS Discourse, open-source robots, local makerspaces, university labs and clubs.

Final advice
  • Build things. A robot that wobbles on your table teaches more than ten chapters.
  • Simulate first, then test early on hardware, and keep a log of what failed.
  • Learn the maths and the hardware. The best roboticists can move between the equation, the code and the screwdriver.
  • Respect safety. Every robot you build can hurt someone; think about it from the first sketch.
