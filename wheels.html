Mobile robots: wheels, drive types and everything else

← Robot list · The mobile robot in 3D · Learn the basics

A mobile robot is a robot that is not fixed to the ground: it moves in its environment. The most important choice when you design one is how it moves. The wheels you pick decide which movements are possible, how well the robot carries loads, how well it knows where it is, and how hard it is to control. This page shows every common wheel type, then lets you drive four different drive types to feel the difference.

1. Wheel types (types de roues)

Engineers sort wheels by two questions: can the wheel steer, and can it slide sideways? Four classes are classic (fixed, steered, castor, omnidirectional), plus balls and tracks.

Suspension (suspension)

  • None (rigid): fine on a perfectly flat floor, with 3 wheels (3 points always touch the floor).
  • Spring-loaded drive wheels: keep the pressure on the floor on bumpy ground. This is very common on differential-drive AMRs.
  • Rocker-bogie: a system of pivoting arms that keeps all 6 wheels on the ground and climbs obstacles as tall as a wheel. It is used by Mars rovers.
  • Mecanum wheels need a suspension: all 4 wheels must touch the floor, otherwise the robot drifts.

2. Drive configurations (configurations de motorisation)

Siegwart and Nourbakhsh describe a robot with two numbers: the degree of mobility δm (how many motions the wheels allow right now, without steering) and the degree of steerability δs (how many wheels you can steer). The degree of manoeuvrability is δM = δm + δs. A robot with δM = 3 can reach any position and heading, but only a robot with δm = 3 can do it instantly: it is holonomic.

4. Know the vocabulary

Holonomic

A robot is holonomic if it can move in any direction at any moment, without first having to turn. A 4-Mecanum robot can slide sideways at once. It is the easiest to program for paths.

Nonholonomic

The robot has more positions to reach than motions available right now. A car cannot slide sideways, so to park beside a wall it must go forward, reverse and turn. Differential and car-like robots are nonholonomic.

ICC

Instantaneous Centre of Curvature: the single point of the plane that does not move at this instant. Every other point turns around it. For wheels that roll without slipping, all the wheel axles must meet at the ICC.

Track width and wheelbase

Track width is the distance between the left and right wheels. Wheelbase is the distance between the front and rear axles. A bigger track makes the robot more stable but turns more slowly.

Odometry and why it drifts

With encoders the robot adds up its small motions: x ← x + v·cosθ·dt, y ← y + v·sinθ·dt, θ ← θ + ω·dt. This is dead reckoning. Errors never go away: wheel slip, a wheel radius that is slightly wrong, uneven floors, and encoder resolution. A small heading error grows into a large position error after a few metres. So robots correct odometry with other sensors.

Localization and mapping

  • Sensor fusion (Kalman filter, EKF): mixes odometry, IMU and other measures into one better estimate.
  • Monte Carlo localization (particle filter, AMCL): the robot knows the map and compares lidar scans with it.
  • SLAM: Simultaneous Localization And Mapping. The robot builds the map while it finds itself in it (lidar SLAM, visual SLAM).
  • Landmarks: QR codes, reflectors, or markers on the floor give an exact position when seen.

Navigation (how a robot gets from A to B)

map  →  localization  →  global planner  →  local planner  →  speed command  →  wheels
                       (A*, Dijkstra)     (DWA, TEB, MPC)    (v, ω)
  • Global planner: finds the whole path on the map (Dijkstra, A*, RRT).
  • Local planner: follows the path and avoids obstacles that were not in the map (DWA, TEB, potential fields).
  • Costmap: a grid that says where it is free, dangerous or forbidden.
  • Recovery behaviours: turn on the spot, back up, or ask for help when the robot is stuck.
  • Fleet management: software that gives jobs to many robots and avoids traffic jams.

Motors, electronics and software

  • Motors: brushed DC with a gearbox (common), brushless (BLDC, also hub motors in the wheel), steppers (cheap, slower).
  • Motor driver: an H-bridge or ESC that turns a command into current. A PID loop on each wheel keeps its speed on target, using the encoder.
  • Brains: a microcontroller (Arduino, STM32, ESP32) for the fast low-level loops, and a computer (Raspberry Pi, Jetson, industrial PC) for navigation.
  • Software: ROS 2 and its navigation stack (Nav2) are the standard tools in research and many products.
  • Battery: Li-ion or LiFePO4 (lead-acid on older machines), with a battery management system (BMS). Many robots go back to a charging dock by themselves.

Safety and standards

  • A safety lidar scanner with protective fields: the robot slows down when a person is near and stops when they are close.
  • Emergency stop, bumpers, speed limits, lights and sounds that warn people.
  • Standards: ISO 3691-4 (driverless industrial trucks), ANSI/RIA R15.08 (industrial mobile robots), and ISO 13849 (safety-related control systems).
